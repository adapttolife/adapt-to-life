How a collage stops being a mood board

This was the risk in what you described, and it is worth naming because it is the whole difference between these and a banner someone made in Canva: a pile of rectangles is a mood board. Four things stop it, and all four are doing visible work here.

Three depths, hard separated. Panels at the back are dim, low-contrast and genuinely blurred; panels at the front are sharp and full brightness. There is no middle ground where you have to decide what to look at. Depth is carried by brightness and blur, never by size — a panel two planes back that is merely smaller still reads as near, which is what makes most collages feel flat.

Atmosphere between the layers, not on top of them. Two haze planes sit inside the stack — one above the back row, one above the middle — so a mid panel is genuinely seen through the air in front of the back row. This is the part you were describing when you said shading and shadow between all the pictures, and it is the part that makes it read as a room rather than a page. Painting the haze on top instead was my first version, and it flattened everything it was meant to separate.

One light source. A warm rake from the lower right. Every panel carries the same hairline warm edge and drops its shadow the same way. The photographs themselves stay black and white — the orange is environment, never a tint on an athlete.

A quiet zone for the type. The wall is authored so its darkest, emptiest region is wherever the headline goes. Contrast is not left to luck, and nothing bright is allowed to cross into it.

And the crop is the whole job. These are 5504×8256 frames with a lot of gym in them, so every panel is cropped to one athlete filling it, faces high. The banner columns needed their own crops — reframing a 3:4 panel into a 1:2 hole crops the middle out of it and lands on a torso, and the first cut of E was seven photographs of nobody's face.

Serif or grotesk

You wrote it as "Your Place in Adaptive Sports" — title case, and the banner you sent is set in a heavy sans. The site is set in a serif today. That is a brand decision rather than a header decision, so rather than guess I put one of each in front of you: D is the site's serif, sentence case, exactly what is live. E is the grotesk, title case, tight, the register a stadium banner is actually set in. Same five words either way.

Less is more, as asked. The eyebrow and the sub-line are gone from both — the wall behind already says "made by athletes, for athletes" better than the words did — and the live drive shrinks from a card to one line.

The recast, and the one thing I had to guess

"I do not want to be the center image, I want Juan to be in the middle" is a casting note, not a layout note — and the first build made it expensive to answer, because the athletes were written into the stylesheet by name. They are not any more. The composition is five slots with fixed geometry, and who stands in each is one line in scripts/build-hero-lab.mjs. Moving somebody is now a one-line change, in all three candidates at once, which is what it should have been the first time.

Juan is s-anchor in all three: front, centre, largest, sharpest, and the only figure allowed in front of everything else. The white-capped athlete moved to s-deep — furthest back, dimmest, off the centre line, still in the picture.

Here is the guess. Nothing in the Drive folder or the site says which of the five is you. The file guide names exactly two people, Juan and Brian, and the athlete it calls Juan is the one who was already in the middle — so I read your note as being about the banner you sent rather than about my build, and I picked the white-capped athlete as you by comparing against the founder portrait on /about. That is an inference, and it is doing real work in the layout, so it should not stay one.

Tell me a letter and I will recast. Two constraints: only A and B are complete figures — whole athlete, whole chair — so only they can hold the front row; C, D and E are cropped at the waist in the source and can only stand behind the floor haze that hides the cut. If you want somebody else in the middle, I need a frame of them that includes the chair.

What changed, and why it should raise more

The header had no people in it. Today the first screen is a sentence about adaptive sport on an empty black field, and the right half of it is nothing. Giving is not moved by a category; it is moved by a person you can picture. Five athletes were sitting in a Drive folder and none of them were above the fold.

The ask is now on the first screen, with a number attached. The live drive — name, goal and the running total — used to sit in a band below the header. It is in the header now, next to Donate, and it hides itself when no drive is open. So the first screen says who, what it costs, and how far along we are, in that order. When it shows there, the duplicate band below stands down; on a phone the header keeps the picture and the band below keeps the money, because the phone screen cannot carry both well. B does the same thing at every width: a centred headline, a sub-line, two buttons AND the strip left about 250px of a 900px laptop for the athletes, which is not enough for anyone to hold the middle.

The picture is a room, not a collage. This is the part that separates it from a banner someone made in Canva, and it is three specific things. Every figure carries the same light: one warm rim from the glow low and right, one cool fill from the left, and nothing gets its own lighting. Depth is contrast, not stacking order — the athletes further back lose contrast, lose brightness, gain a little blur and sink into a floor haze, which is what twenty feet of gym air actually does. And every figure has a contact shadow on a shared floor line, because a cut-out without one is floating no matter how well it is masked. Then the whole group drifts a few pixels against your pointer and sinks slightly as you scroll, so it reads as a space rather than a picture.

One thing worth knowing about the files

The cut-outs in Drive are banner outline mattes: the silhouette around each athlete is right, but the holes are not cut — the grey court between the wheel spokes and under the chair frame is still solid. On a dark header that renders as a pale rectangle behind the athlete's legs, and it is the single detail that tells a viewer the picture is pasted together. So the court is keyed out here, per photograph, because the two front athletes ride different chairs: one is matte black against a smooth bright floor, and one is silver, which is exactly as smooth and as bright as the floor it sits on.

Worth saying plainly: I tried the obvious fix first and it was wrong. The local background-removal model produces a beautiful matte and deletes the wheelchair, because it is a human segmentation model. For these athletes the chair is the athlete. That is written into the build script so nobody reaches for it again.

Nothing about any athlete is altered, nothing is generated, and no photograph is mirrored. An earlier cut of B flipped the right-hand athlete so the group looked inward from both sides; that is not a design move on a page about real people, and the other athlete's shirt reads Different body, same drive, which mirroring would turn into nonsense. Both keep the orientation they were photographed in.

On a phone

Most of the people who will ever see this header will see it on a phone, so the first build was a failure there: it pushed every athlete below the fold and handed the majority of the traffic a wall of type. All three candidates now converge at that width — one screen, headline and Donate in the top half, two athletes standing in the bottom 40%.

Both new candidates get their own phone composition rather than a thinned-down desktop one. A wall authored for a wide box does not survive being squeezed into a tall one, so D drops to six large panels filling the bottom half and E to two full-height columns. Dropping panels is the only honest way to thin a collage — shrinking all nineteen turns nineteen photographs into nineteen smudges.

What is machine-checked, not eyeballed

A header is judged by eye, but three things about it are not opinions and a screenshot cannot show them. scripts/check-hero-lab.mjs runs all five candidates at six widths from 320 to 1920 and fails the build on any of these.

  • No sideways scroll at any width, on any candidate — the panels and the athletes bleed past both edges on purpose, inside a clipping section.
  • The headline and the Donate button are above the fold at every width, including 320px.
  • The picture costs 489 KB on D, 472 KB on E and 502 KB on A, against a 620 KB budget — nineteen photographs inside a budget that was set for five, because panel size follows depth rather than importance.
  • Reduced motion lands on the finished frame, not the pre-entrance one. No drift, no fade-in, everything visible.
  • No console errors and no missing files on any candidate at any width.
