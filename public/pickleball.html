What we’re building

More than one trip.

The US Open is one stop. The bigger goal is an ecosystem for the adaptive sports community to play pickleball in the Chicago area, built with clubs, organizations and coaches.

Trying it is one thing. Playing every week, training every month and competing is another. In a wheelchair, every step costs more, starting with the chair: a sport chair runs $4,000 to $6,000, and insurance doesn’t cover it. Here are the real numbers for each level, and what keeps it going.

  1. 01

    Play anytime

    $15a night, per athlete

    Open play and drop-ins at clubs across the area, with no membership and nothing to lock in. At our partner club, we cover the drop-in, so every athlete plays free. $225 covers up to 15 for a night.

    Kept going by night sponsors, host clubs and volunteers

  2. 02

    Train to compete

    $129a month, per athlete

    Memberships for athletes who train regularly so they can travel to tournaments. One session a week isn’t enough: Juan trains 16 hours a week. A membership is $129 a month for unlimited sessions, so a year of training is $1,548.

    Kept going by membership sponsors

    Sponsor an athlete

  3. 03

    Compete

    $1,000a tournament trip, per athlete

    A season on the road to the US Open, then the Chicago Classic once it has a date. A tournament trip runs about $1,000 an athlete for entry fees, travel and an accessible room. The US Open, the road’s last stop, has cost about $3,500.

    Kept going by tournament sponsors, entry fees and donors to the fund

    See the road

It takes skill and a little luck to get to the US Open. It shouldn’t take money.

The US Open picks players by lottery. If an athlete’s name comes up, money shouldn’t be the reason they stay home. We want the best of the best in Naples.

Open Court clubs

Places to play, built together.

Through ambassadors like Juan Ortiz, we help clubs, organizations and coaches build and manage adaptive sports programs, so the adaptive sports community has places to play across the area. Some sessions have a sport chair to try or a coach on hand, and some are just open play. We grow each one little by little. It’s the Open Court Model at work.

Across the region

We also support adaptive programs across the region: coaching that helped the Wisconsin Adaptive Sports Association (WASA) start pickleball, and work with the Lincolnway Special Recreation Association (LWSRA) on clinics in the south suburbs. And we partner with Chicago Adaptive Sports, a Chicagoland nonprofit where our first ambassador, Juan Ortiz, serves on the board. Those relationships keep growing.
